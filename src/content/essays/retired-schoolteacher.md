There is a man in a Kolkata para who taught History at a government school for thirty-one years and now heads the neighbourhood cultural committee. He has a pension, a scooter, and a flat his father bought. When there is a wedding, a funeral, a Durga Puja budget dispute, or a family that needs talking to, he is the one who is consulted.

Two lanes away lives a software engineer, forty-one, who earns roughly seven times what the teacher ever did. He owns a car, an apartment he is still paying for, and a photo album documenting the sixth family holiday in Singapore. When there is a wedding, a funeral, a Puja budget dispute or a family that needs talking to, nobody calls him.

Neither of them finds this strange. Everyone in that neighbourhood could tell you whom they pay heed to, and nobody would think the answer required explanation.

I found that it required a great deal of explanation. So I built a model.

## The distinction is century-old

Max Weber separated *Klasse* (class), which he defined by market situation — property, skills, earning capacity, life chances — from *Stand* (status), which is prestige a community actually confers, expressed through conduct and style of life rather than through balance sheets. His sharpest line: the propertied and the propertyless can belong to the same status group. The teacher and the engineer are not anomalous. They are Weber's distinction wearing kurtas.

Money is not the basis of social honour, and its naked display can actively corrode it, as anyone who has watched a man mention his salary at a family dinner will recognise.

This is where Pierre Bourdieu becomes particularly useful. Economic, cultural and social resources do not become status simply by existing. They acquire symbolic capital when they are perceived and recognised as legitimate within a particular social field.

That distinction matters. A degree is a credential. It becomes symbolic capital when the relevant people regard it as impressive. A network is a collection of relationships. It becomes symbolic capital when those relationships confer influence, access or authority. And the conversion is not automatic.

Bourdieu's concept of *méconnaissance*, or misrecognition, is useful here: symbolic capital works partly because its underlying economic or social origins are not foregrounded. Prestige is experienced as legitimacy, competence, taste or honour rather than as a visible transaction. This helps explain why *claiming* status and *possessing* status are not necessarily the same thing.

What Weber gives us is the distinction. What he does not give us is the mechanism. He tells us the two orders diverge. He does not tell us what converts one into the other, or why two people with almost identical resources end up with wildly different amounts of standing.

That is the gap.

## The class trajectory equation

Start with the familiar half, where a person is going. I present it as:

**CT = C × HC × G**

**C** is capital. Family wealth, property, financial security, and the advantages available at the starting point. It does its most consequential work early, at the moment someone decides which institution a seventeen-year-old will attend.

**HC** is human capital. Education, skills, qualifications, productive competence. The useful measure is not how many degrees someone has, but how effectively those qualifications convert into productive capability and opportunity. A master's in a field that has limited commercial viability is a real credential with a poor conversion rate.

**G** is geography. The opportunity structure of the place where those resources are deployed — labour markets, wages, institutions, professional networks, access to higher-value work.

Score each on a scale of one to ten. Nobody scores zero on anything; even the most constrained person has some capital, some skill, some labour market. The multiplication is not there to produce catastrophes. It is there because these terms condition each other's returns.

A given education pays differently depending on where it is deployed. A given amount of capital pays differently depending on the human capital available to convert it into something. Two software engineers with the same skills, one in Munich and one in Lucknow, do not have the same trajectory. Geography is not a backdrop. It changes the coefficient on everything else.

This is also why the exceptional cases behave the way they do.

Someone who clears the GMAT or the UPSC from a family with very little capital is not breaking the model. They are compensating within it: an extreme score on one term, offsetting a low score on another, to reach a product that most people with their starting position never reach. The multiplicative form is precisely what makes that route rare and punishing rather than impossible. If the terms merely added up, a strong HC would quietly cover a weak C, and we would expect far more people to make that jump than actually do.

So CT is a model of *potential and direction*. It asks:

> Given this person's resources and opportunity structure, where is their socioeconomic life likely to go?

And it answers that question reasonably well. It just does not answer the other one.

## The social-standing equation

Class Trajectory ≠ Social Standing (or simply status)

**SS = f(Co, Sc, Fu)**

**Co** is competence. Defined narrowly: demonstrated capacity to discharge consequential responsibilities reliably and effectively. Track record, not potential. This keeps it measurable, and it keeps it separate from education and job title.

**Sc** is socio-cultural capital. Influence, network, knowledge, taste — the things that make a person legible and valuable to a group.

**Fu** is function. Socially consequential action taken on behalf of a group, usually at the moments when the group constitutes itself as a group. Weddings, funerals, disputes, committees, crises.

Notice what has happened. Money and education have not disappeared from the model. They have moved upstream. They create capacity; they make certain trajectories possible. But social standing is an output of a different process. A person can have money, qualifications and an impressive trajectory without becoming particularly consequential to the people around them.

For resources to become authority, they have to be converted into attributes the group recognises: competence, usefulness, responsibility, influence, trust. The trajectory tells us what someone has accumulated. The standing tells us what that accumulation has become.

## The Geography Paradox

Migration raises class trajectory. Better labour markets, currency differentials, credential premiums. The mechanism is obvious and well documented.

It also frequently lowers standing in the community of origin. Not because anyone disapproves. Because of four separate things, and they are worth separating, since "they're far away" is a description rather than a mechanism.

**Absence.** Function cannot be discharged as effectively remotely. The collective occasion is physically located.

**Reduced observability.** Achievements the group cannot witness cannot be recognised by it. Recognition requires witnesses, and witnesses require proximity.

**Field drift.** The migrant accumulates capital in a field the group does not participate in. There is no counterparty.

**Illegibility.** Even reported achievements become difficult to price. Learning that someone teaches AI workflow design in Berlin produces a polite nod, not a re-ranking.

So, heuristically:

**G ↑ → CT ↑**

while, under fairly common conditions:

**G ↑ → SS ↓**

The same variable, loaded oppositely. Which produces an outcome that looks paradoxical and is not: a person can become richer, better educated, more mobile and objectively more successful while becoming less socially central to the place they came from. And someone else can remain entirely ordinary on every trajectory variable while becoming one of the most important people in it.

Neither result breaks the model. They are evidence that we have been measuring two different things and calling both of them success.

## Status is converted

Status is not merely accumulated. It is converted. Which brings us to the mechanism Weber left out.

Economic capital can become education. Education can become occupational opportunity. Opportunity can become income. Income can become lifestyle.

None of that automatically becomes social honour.

For that, something has to happen: a person has to become competent, discerning, present, trusted or otherwise recognised as valuable by a specific group. Two people can acquire similar resources; yet one becomes admired, and the other merely affluent. The difference is not the resources. It is the *conversion rate*.

Bourdieu's symbolic capital is resources that acquire prestige, honour or authority and which are recognised as legitimate within a field. The load-bearing word is *recognised*.

A qualification is not automatically prestigious everywhere. A profession is not automatically impressive everywhere. A luxury object is not automatically a status symbol everywhere. Capital becomes socially valuable only where there is a field capable of valuing it. One can announce that they are important. It is much more convincing when everyone else behaves as though they are. Bourdieu calls this process *consecration*: legitimacy conferred by people or institutions whose own position gives them the authority to recognise value. This means recognition cannot be self-generated.

Capital is what one possesses. Symbolic capital is what the field recognises that possession to mean. A person can be extraordinarily competent, and if the relevant field never observes it, the conversion is close to zero. This is not unfairness. It is the mechanism working as designed.

## The fifth Ferrari

This is where status gets slightly funny.

Suppose one person in a neighbourhood owns a Ferrari. That is information. Suppose ten people do. That is a demographic fact.

Adding the fifth Ferrari communicates less than the first, because its differentiating power has collapsed. The same applies to almost every conventional signal. If everyone in an affluent professional cohort holds a master's degree, the degree establishes membership in the cohort and distinguishes nobody within it. If everyone travels internationally, another international holiday adds nothing. If everyone wears gold at weddings, gold stops carrying information about relative position. Symbolic value isn't determined simply by the quantity of capital possessed. It depends on what that capital means within the field.

A rough proposition about status signalling is:

**Status value ∝ differentiation × legitimacy**

The more common a characteristic becomes, the less it differentiates. The more the field treats it as a legitimate basis for honour, the more efficiently it converts. This produces a specific effect in upwardly mobile groups. As prosperity spreads through the reference group, prosperity stops working as a status differentiator — not because the group has become spiritually superior, but because the signal has saturated. The scarce currencies then move elsewhere.

## The field sets the exchange rate

There is no universal status score.

A founder in California, a cultural organiser in Kolkata, an academic in London and a small-town politician in Pijao hold entirely different forms of recognised capital. The same person can be powerful in one field and unremarkable in another, and both readings are correct, because each field has its own currency and there is no exchange window between them. A person's professional standing in Berlin may be entirely real. It may simply not translate into standing in Kolkata. Different field. Different currency. Different exchange rate.

So the question is never:

> How high-status is this person?

It is:

> How much recognised capital does a person hold in a particular field?

That sounds like a small correction. It changes the whole analysis.

## Two systems

Class trajectory is about resources and opportunity. Social standing/status is about recognised value.

The first asks where a person can go. The second asks how much they matter there. The variables that answer those questions overlap far less than we assume. Capital, human capital and geography can produce extraordinary mobility. But once prosperity is widespread, additional prosperity produces diminishing status returns, and standing migrates towards whatever is still scarce. Competence. Responsibility. Presence. Judgment. Trust.

So the person with the most resources may not be the person with the most status, and the person with the highest status may not have the most resources. They are not competing at the same game. They are playing two different ones. Which is why the fifth Ferrari gets a glance rather than envy, and why a retired school teacher running the neighbourhood cultural committee is the one everyone actually listens to.

Getting ahead and becoming important are not the same achievement. They merely happen to occupy the same street.
