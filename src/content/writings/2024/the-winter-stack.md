Every year I start with a list. It is always eleven books, which is a number I have arrived at by discarding larger ambitions, and every year I get through between five and seven of them, and the four that remain go into a box that I do not look at until the following February.

For a long time I treated this as a character flaw. The list is the plan; the plan is the discipline; failing to complete it means something about the person. Then I started keeping the leftover books instead of binning them, which is a change of four years standing, and the box turned out to be the more interesting document.

## What the pile records

Sorted by the reason I did not finish them, the leftovers divide cleanly into three piles, and the reasons are not about willpower at all.

The first pile is books I was not in the right season for. I have been reading the same novel for eleven months because every time I open it I am in a different mood from the one it needs. This has nothing to do with discipline. It is the reading equivalent of trying to cook something in a room that is too bright.

The second pile is books that turned out to be wrong. Not bad — wrong, for me, at this moment. There are a great many excellent books that are a bad fit for a person in a particular winter, and the pile is honest about this in a way that a completed reading list never is.

> A list you finished tells you what you could get through. A list you abandoned tells you what you actually were.

The third pile is the embarrassing one and it is by far the largest: books I did not finish because I was not reading them. I was reading them in the sense that they were open on the table in November, which is not reading.

## The February box

So every February I open the box, and I do not feel guilty, and I do something more useful than starting a new list.

I take the four books out and I read them properly. Not all at once — one, on a walk, at a hundred words an hour of attention, which is the only pace at which I finish anything.

It turns out that between November and February, my season has changed, and the books that were wrong in November are frequently exactly right in February. The same book, the same reader, three months apart. Nothing was wrong with the book. Something was wrong with the room I was in.

## What the eleven is for

I still make the list. I would not stop. It is the only part of the method that involves deciding in advance, and deciding in advance is genuinely useful, because it makes you choose eleven books rather than reading whatever is nearest.

But the list is no longer a promise. It is a room with the furniture pushed to the walls. I expect to fill it, and I expect to leave part of it empty, and I have learned that the part I leave empty is the part that will be waiting for me in February, when I have changed into somebody who wants to read it.
