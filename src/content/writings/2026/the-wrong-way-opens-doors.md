Andy Warhol once told a writer that he had never sat down to write a novel, and that he had already written one. He was not being mysterious. He was being precise. The book existed, and the novel did not, and the difference between the two things turned out to matter enormously to how the book was made.

The novel, in the ordinary sense, is a thing you go and do. You sit down. You make a shape in advance and then you fill it. You know the ending before you have earned it. Warhol's book was the other operation entirely. It came from a conversation, and a recording, and a list, and a person telephoning him at two in the morning to say one more true thing. The order was not imposed from outside. The order arrived, and then it was sorted.

## Two ways of beginning

Almost every process book describes the first. The clean table, the known shape, the sequence of drafts. It is a true description of a real way to work, and it is how I was taught. It is also, I have come to think, the more lonely of the two.

The second way is harder to describe and easier to keep. You gather what is true. You write down who said what, in what order, with what pauses. You do not yet know what the thing is called. Then, one afternoon, a name arrives for it, and the gathering turns out to have been a shape all along.

I have had three or four pieces that arrived the second way and roughly a hundred that arrived the first, and I trust the second more, because it does not depend on me being in a good mood at a desk. It depends on me being a decent listener for long enough.

> The shape was never the hard part. The hard part was staying in the room until the shape showed up.

## What the wrong way costs

It costs discipline, which is the first objection anyone makes. If you are not imposing an order, who is? The answer is that you are imposing one — a much later one, and a much more accurate one. The discipline moves from the front of the process to the back of it. You are not allowed to be vague at the end, where it counts.

It also costs nerve, for a different reason than you would expect. It is not nerve to begin without a plan. It is nerve to publish something whose plan you could not have drawn in advance, and to trust the reader to meet you halfway.

## A small practice

Here is what I have started doing, which took four years to arrive at and costs nothing.

I keep a document for each piece. Into it go: quotations with the date and the place they were said, the weather, the odd turns of phrase, and anything I cannot account for. Nothing in it is written to be used. The document is not a draft and it is not an outline. It is a room with the door shut. Nothing in it is for sale.

When the piece is ready to be written, I open the document and I read it straight through, and I let it tell me the order.

Sometimes there is no order in there. Then it is not ready, and I go back to gathering, and that is a perfectly good outcome too. The room is not on a schedule. It fills when there is enough in it.
