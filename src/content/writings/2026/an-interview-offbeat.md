We met on a Tuesday in February, on the first floor of a building in Worcester, in the room where Gerry Badcock keeps his eight-track machines. There are eleven of them, and he knows what each one is for. That is the first thing you learn about him: the machines are not a collection, they are a working tool, and he resents anyone who treats them as nostalgia.

**PR: You built OffBeat. Records by people nobody else would release.**

GB: That is roughly it, and I would put a more generous version first. We released records that were good and that no one had room for. There is a difference. Nonsense has its own supply.

**PR: The label is known for the dead band.**

GB: We never called it that. It is a sound you get when the format cannot hold the information and something gets lost. Do you remember the hiss on a cassette? That is a version of it. What we were doing was to stop pretending the loss was not there and put it in the mix on purpose. Once you accept the loss, the loss is a colour.

> You cannot engineer a perfect copy of something. You can only decide what to do with what the machine took.

**PR: And the machinery is eleven eight-tracks rather than a computer.**

GB: The computer is a machine that does the obvious thing at enormous length. I wanted a machine that did one thing badly and let me hear it. There is a sound you can get off eight-track that you cannot get off anything made since, and it comes from the limitation, and I am not going to pretend that is a moral position. It is a preference. But preferences are what records are made of.

**PR: You were also a musician before any of this.**

GB: Badly. I played in a band that had one good idea and took it as far as it would go, which was about eleven minutes. The label came out of that. When you make something by accident and then cannot put it down, you have to build a place to put it.

**PR: Did you think it would last?**

GB: No, and I was right. It lasted about four years, which is a very long time for a record label. Long enough to put out thirty-odd things and to be wrong about most of them. I would rather have four years of a thing I believed in than thirty years of a thing I had planned.

We had been talking for three hours when the light went. He did not turn it on; he got up, put a record on the eight-track, and we sat in the dark and listened to it, which is the only reasonable thing to do in a room like that. The hiss in that room was not background. It was the point of the building.
