Allen Ginsberg was born in 1926, which means this year he turns a hundred, and the strange thing about a centenary is that it invites you to check a body of work against a date and find that the date means nothing.

I went back to *Howl* this spring, in one sitting, which is the only honest way to read it. The famous opening — that everything is sacred, that everything is broken, that the typewriter jams on the cracked paper of the human brain — sounded to me like a young man being loud because he had found out that being quiet was not available to him.

It is not that. It is a technique, and the technique is that the poem refuses to be consoled. Not angrily. That is the part I had missed. Ginsberg is not denouncing anything in that first section. He is simply declining to move to a place where the suffering has been made tidy, and then he proceeds at speed, for three pages, without once slowing down to check whether you are keeping up.

## The list as a form of tenderness

Here is the structural thing. *Howl* is a long list, and lists are not usually where you go for comfort. But read the list closely and you see that it is an inventory of what he actually saw — the smell, the money, the institutional corridors, the specific cheapness of a particular afternoon.

A list of particulars is a form of care. It says: this happened, and it happened to somebody, and I am not going to let it dissolve into a mood. The famous admonition to the reader, *whoever you are, are you mad enough*, is not a boast. It is an invitation to check your own nerve.

> A poem that only lists is a catalogue. A poem that lists the way a witness lists is a different instrument entirely.

## What does not age

The parts of the book that have not aged are the parts where he is specific about a body. Ginsberg is unusually comfortable writing about his own body, and about other people's, in a register that is neither confession nor display but a kind of accounting. He is keeping track. He wants the reader to know that this happened inside a person and not merely inside a decade.

The parts that have aged are the manifestos. Not because they were wrong, but because a declaration that the system is mad is easier to make than an account of what the system did to you on a particular Tuesday. Readers have gotten better at asking the second question.

That, on reflection, is most of what a centenary is for. Not celebration. A reassessment of which parts of the work have earned their place and which parts were only ever the loudspeaker. *Howl* survives because the witness is more durable than the slogan, and because in a hundred years nobody has been able to make the poem sound like a period piece, which is the highest compliment available to a work of art.
