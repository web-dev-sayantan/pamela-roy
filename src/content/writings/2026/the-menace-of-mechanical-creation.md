The loudest objection to writing with a machine is usually moral, and the moral objection is not the interesting one. The interesting objection is structural, and it predates the machine by about two centuries.

A first draft is a record of what somebody found difficult. That is its whole function. Nobody writes a first draft in order to have got it right, and nobody shows one to a reader. The draft is the room with the furniture pushed to the walls: it is where you find out what the piece is about by writing badly until you cannot avoid the subject. It is expensive, slow, and full of the wrong turns, and the wrong turns are the point. They are the evidence of the thinking.

## What the tool actually does

A generator does not have difficulty. It has a distribution. Given a prompt, it produces the most likely continuation, and the most likely continuation is a competent middle. Middle is what a draft is made of, which is why generated first drafts read so strangely: they have all the texture of a draft and none of the record of one. Nothing in them was hard.

The consequence is that the discovery step is missing. A writer who opens an empty page is, among other things, asking a question. A writer who opens a good suggestion is reading an answer to a question nobody posed.

> The test I would put to any drafting tool: does it make the writer's next decision harder or easier? Not the words. The decision.

## Where it is genuinely useful

I want to be fair here, because the tool is useful and I use it.

- Getting a piece you have abandoned onto the page, badly, so that you can see what you were avoiding.
- Producing three structurally different versions of the same middle section, which is a way of finding out that you have only been trying one shape.
- Reading back to yourself, at speed, in a register that is not your own. The ear catches things the eye forgives.

None of these is composition. All of them are useful. The confusion comes from calling any of this writing.

## The inheritance problem

A ghostwriter's deliverable is a finished object that the client signs. That creates an obligation that no tool has any concept of: somebody has to stand behind every sentence and be able to say why it is there. If a sentence arrived by the most probable route, the honest answer to *why is this here* is *because it is likely*, and that is not an answer you can give a publisher.

The rule I have settled on is narrow and I would keep it narrow. Use the machine to destroy drafts, to multiply options, and to read aloud. Do not use it to produce the sentences you will be responsible for. The sentences you will be responsible for have to be ones you can account for, and accounting for a sentence is the entire discipline.

The technology is new. The inheritance is not, and it is not going to become less of a problem as the tools get better. It is the same problem it was when the publisher's clerk and the author's secretary both knew which sentences were the dangerous ones.
