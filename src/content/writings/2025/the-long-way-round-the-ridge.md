The map offers a nine-mile path from the car park to the bothy. I have taken it maybe twice. The ridge route is fourteen, and it is the one I do.

This is not an argument about fitness and it is not about scenery, though the scenery is better. The nine miles is a line between two points, drawn by somebody who cared about the contour lines and not at all about what is between them. It goes past the reservoir, which is fine, and under the pylons, which is not, and arrives when it says it will.

> A straight line is a claim about the world that no landscape has yet agreed to.

The fourteen is the same two points connected by a person who was walking, which is to say connected by everything they were willing to stop for. It goes over the col because the col was there. It loses half an hour somewhere in the plantation because the light came through in a particular way and somebody had to stand in it.

## What the extra miles buy

I have started to think of the difference as a kind of attention budget. The nine-mile path spends nothing. It is optimised, and optimisation means that the walk stops being an activity and becomes a transit. Nothing on it can surprise you, which is also to say that nothing on it can be the reason you remember the day.

The ridge spends the whole budget and gets you there tired, at a worse time, in worse light. And you have a story, which is not the same as an experience, but is close enough that I stopped correcting myself about it.

The season matters more than I expected. In summer the long way is just longer. From October it is a different proposition, because the light leaves at four and the col takes ninety minutes in the dark, and the walking stops being a preference and turns into a small problem you have to solve. A nine-mile path would have let me be home for dinner. I was not home for dinner.

## The bothy

The bothy has a stove, a slate floor, and a view of nothing in particular, which is what the good ones have. I got there at five past six, in the dark, and put the stove on, and sat down.

Nobody was there. That is the other thing about the long route: it is a commitment, and the commitment is honoured by the people who do not come. A crowded bothy is a booking. An empty one, at dusk, at the end of fourteen miles you chose over nine, is evidence of something you did earlier in the day.

I ate, and wrote down nothing, and went to sleep at eight because there is nothing to do in a bothy at eight. In the morning the reservoir was below and the pylons were a thread, and I took the nine-mile path home, because I had earned it and because walking back along something you have already walked is one of the few pleasures that costs nothing.

The fourteen miles is not the better walk. The nine is. I simply prefer to have spent the five.
