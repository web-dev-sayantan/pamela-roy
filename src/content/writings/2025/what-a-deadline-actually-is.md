A deadline is not a date. A date is where the deadline appears to be. A deadline is a decision that was made some time before that date, by somebody, about what is not going to happen.

Almost nobody in a project agrees on this, and it causes most of the trouble I am called in to fix. The writer thinks a deadline is a target. The editor thinks it is a constraint. The publisher thinks it is a fact of nature. All three of them are carrying a different object with the same name.

## The decision is made early

Somebody chose the date, and the choosing was the work. It came out of a season, a print cycle, a marketing plan, an amount of money, or a habit. Often several. But at some point a person who had the authority decided how much time this thing was getting, and everything after that was people trying to live up to a decision that was never explained to them.

When a project goes wrong, the useful question is not *why is it late*. It is *what did we decide, and when, and who decided*. Almost always the date arrived as an atmosphere rather than as a fact. Everyone knew. Nobody said it. Everybody adjusted, and the adjusting took the time the writing needed.

> A date on a calendar is an outcome. A deadline is an agreement, and agreements can be renegotiated before they are met.

## Renegotiating is the whole skill

The only genuinely useful move in a slipping project is to go back to the person who set the date and tell them what it now requires. This is uncomfortable and it is nearly always cheaper than the alternative, because the alternative is that you absorb the slip privately by writing worse.

There are exactly three honest outcomes to that conversation:

1. The date moves. Simple, occasionally possible, usually later than it needs to be.
2. The scope moves. A chapter, a section, an interview, gets cut. This is normal and should not be treated as failure.
3. The quality moves. The piece gets submitted as it is, and everybody knows that is what happened.

What does not work is the fourth outcome, the one where nobody says anything and the work quietly gets worse. I have watched it happen and I have done it. It is the only one of the four that leaves you with nothing afterwards — not a late book, but a thin one, and no memory of having agreed to it.

## Write it down

The cheap intervention, the one I recommend in every first meeting, is a paragraph. Before anybody starts, write down what is being promised, to whom, by when, and what is being given up to make that true.

It is a small document. It is the only artefact from a project that nobody ever argues about later, because it settled the argument before the heat was in it. Every expensive conversation I have ever had about a deadline was a conversation that should have been a paragraph in the first month.

The furniture gets pushed to the walls in the beginning, not the end. That is the whole of the craft, and it applies to the calendar as much as it applies to the page.
