There is a speed at which you can write that produces pages, and a speed at which you can write that produces work. They are not the same speed, and only one of them looks like effort from the outside.

I want to defend the second one, because it is the only one I have ever used, and because the whole industry is organised around praising the first.

## The appearance

Slow writing, seen from the next desk, looks like nothing happening. The document does not grow visibly. There is a long stretch where the person is reading, or walking, or sitting with a pencil doing apparently nothing at all, and then there is a bad morning, and then the draft appears at the end of the month in a state of completeness that seems, frankly, suspicious.

I have had this conversation with editors approximately once a fortnight for twenty years. The question is always the same: *have you started?* And the answer I give — yes, constantly, not visibly — is not believed, because the only legible evidence of writing is the appearance of words, and the appearance of words is not what writing is.

> The work happens in the part where you cannot yet say what you are making. Words are the evidence that it happened, not the thing itself.

## The cost, honestly stated

I do not think it is free. Two things are true at once and both should be said.

First, slow writing is a luxury of a kind. It requires slack in the schedule, or somebody absorbing the cost, and it is not available to everyone. A writer with a deadline in nine weeks does not get to be slow, and it is unhelpful to romanticise a pace that depends on having room.

Second, it can fail. There are a great many rooms with the furniture pushed to the walls and nothing ever written in them, and a person who is slow is not automatically deep. Slowness is a precondition for a certain kind of work. It is not a guarantee of it, and it is not a virtue in itself, and there is a version of slow writing that is simply fear that has dressed itself up as method.

## What actually helps

Since I have no time for mysticism, here is what I do, which is not mysterious.

- Keep a document per piece. Add to it for weeks before writing anything. No sentences, only material.
- Write the last paragraph early, and do not look at it again. It is a bearing, not a target.
- Read two books in the field. Not for material. To learn what the thing sounds like at full length.
- Write badly, on purpose, at speed, for one hour a week, in a document nobody will see. This is the only warm-up that works, and it works because it makes the day where you are not writing with stopwatch energy bearable.

None of this is a system. It is a set of habits that have survived contact with a real career, and I hold them loosely.

## The only defence

The argument for slowness is not that it is better, or more authentic, or more in keeping with some imagined tradition of serious work. It is more modest than that.

Slowness is the only way I have found to notice that I am wrong. Speed is very good at producing the thing you already thought. It is exceptionally good at it, and it will do so under any deadline, which is precisely the problem. The moment of finding out that the piece is about something else is rare, quiet, and does not announce itself, and if you are moving too fast to be in the room when it happens, you will not know it happened. You will simply have written the thing you already thought, and it will have taken four months, and it will be fine.
