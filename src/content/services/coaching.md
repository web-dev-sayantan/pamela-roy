I coach, and I will not pretend it is a different job from writing. I read a great deal, I ask a great many questions, and I mostly try to get you to say the thing you already know in a form that survives contact with a reader.

## The hour

It is an hour, once a week, and you set the day. There is homework, and the homework is usually to write less than you think you should. The work happens between the hours, in the room where nobody is watching, which is the part of the process that coaching is actually made of.

I will ask you to read something aloud. I mean this literally. Reading a paragraph aloud, at speed, in the room, will find every sentence you are hiding behind, and it takes four minutes.

## The notes

After every session I write up what we covered and send it within two days, while it is still useful. Roughly a page. Nothing in it is new material — it is a record of what you said, arranged so you can see it.

At the end of each month there is a longer letter: where the work is, what has stalled, and what I would do about it. This is the part clients tell me they read twice.

## The honesty clause

I will tell you when something is not working. Sometimes the honest answer is that the book is not the book, and the honest answer is occasionally that it should not be written. I have said the second thing perhaps four times in eleven years, and in three of those cases the client came back a year later with the same idea and a better reason for it, which I was glad about.

I do not do developmental editing of other people's books at this rate, and I do not read fiction manuscripts for pleasure. Coaching is about your own work.
